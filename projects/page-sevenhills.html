Hidden-Traitor Board Game — Class Project

SEVEN HILLS

Role
Game design · TTS scripting (Lua)
Team
5 designers
Timeline
Feb – May 2024
Format
Board game · 4–7 players

Seven Hills is a hidden-traitor board game for 4 to 7 players. Everyone plays a Roman god trying to save the seven hills of Rome from monsters and disasters, but one or two players are secretly traitors who win if four hills fall. Threats are played face down, so players can only hint at what they saw, and the table votes on who the traitor is after rounds 3 and 6.

We made it as a team of five for a game design class. The original pitch was Ada Miller’s, and the whole project was very collective. I worked on game design and documentation, helping cement the mechanics after the pitch. I helped brainstorm health for the hills, the Pokémon-inspired weaknesses, disaster tokens, and hero strength that wears down with each fight. I also modeled and scripted the hill temples in our Tabletop Simulator build, where most of our playtesting happened.

  • Tabletop Simulator — temples I modeled and scripted to track each hill’s health
  • Elemental counters — which threats beat which, plus a +2 matchup bonus
  • Hero cards — powerful face-up cards that fight the monsters on a hill
  • Disaster tokens — new threats that spawn on empty hills

Playtesting

  • All three testers who filled out our first form had skimmed the rules, and only one was sure how to set up the board. We added a reference table of the gods to the rules sheet, and the Elemental Defeats table gave players the weaknesses at a glance.
  • Early games were stacked against the traitor, even by the loyal players’ account. We started more cards face down and hid every disaster at the start, and later tests found the balance better with most hill cards hidden.
  • In one test nobody used their god powers. We made god cards usable at any point in the game, and when we tested that in late April, they felt more useful.

Takeaways

  • Most of our testing happened in Tabletop Simulator, and we didn’t playtest enough overall. Players told us the small card text got in the way and that the game might play better as a physical board game, so next time I’d test a physical copy earlier and more often.
  • A few misunderstandings about how parts of the game were supposed to work weren’t caught right away, and they led to major design changes later. Asking more questions and meeting more often early on would have caught them sooner.
  • We counted type matchups as one of the game’s strengths, but they weren’t intuitive and they added math to monster fights. Next time I’d make the matchup sheet clearer.
