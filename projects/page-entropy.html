State of Entropy is a narrative-driven, zero-gravity cosmic stalker horror game made in Unity. Movement is survival, and mastering it is the only way to escape an intelligent, relentless presence hunting you through a derelict spacecraft.

We developed it through the Magic Makers Program, a co-op program at RIT’s MAGIC Spell Studios. In this environment, our production was treated as a professional, industry-grade product, with stakeholders and expectations for the term. It started as a class prototype and grew to a team of 13, with more than 20 different members over its development. I co-founded the project and have been one of its leads ever since, both during our time with MAGIC and after.

  • The Geist (the alien) — dithered shader, IK stepping, soft-body physics, eye-stem IK
  • Zero-G gameplay — floating objects, grab & throw, air breaches
  • Level design — blocked out level one, reworked it with the project lead after playtests, and modeled most of its rooms
  • Tech art — procedural blood decals, room light groups, curved in-world terminals

Development

The project’s full development timeline, from the first class prototype to the current build, with my process laid out at each stage. Each chapter covers what that phase was, what changed, and what I worked on.

Early prototype

It started as a class project with problems all over its gameplay, visuals and development. Work across the semester was scattered and undisciplined, and most of what you see here was rushed in over the last week or two. We applied to the Magic Makers Program with it and didn’t get in because it wasn’t polished enough.

I helped originate the concept and its core mechanics, prototyped the doors and the object throwing and physics, and built the entire map, including the layout, modeling and colliders.

Final prototype

Confident in the idea, we spent the next 3–4 months of the semester working on our own to overhaul it into something we could present to MAGIC. We finalized more assets, fixed bugs, made the gameplay clearer and built up the atmosphere to match the game we wanted to make. This is the prototype that got us into the Magic Makers Program, and in June 2025 we entered the program and moved into full production.

I kept building out the station map, including its layout, modeling and colliders, along with the doors and object physics.

Graybox

Getting into Magic Makers meant turning a tech demo into a full game, so we leaned into zero gravity and into making the station feel real. Level one is linear, but it’s built to feel like you’re exploring, with verticality and lighting that points the way. Halls and some core rooms have no floor or true up and down, while checkpoint rooms keep a floor and ceiling so you can get your bearings. Playtesting showed our mechanics were hard to pick up, so level one also became the tutorial. Its scripted beats teach the mechanics, hint at the alien and show what happened before you woke up.

I built the first blockout and the primitive prefabs the team prototyped with, designed the landmark spaces, and kept reworking the layout through playtests.

Final build

Every area went from graybox to concept to finished assets, all in the same art style. Based on player feedback, we reworked the player controller and dropped realism wherever it hurt gameplay. Narrative and story beats now drive progression through the level.

I modeled and laid out each core room by hand, working from Tobin Pedersen’s concept art, except the dining hall, which Hayden Bush-Resko made with my help. The halls use a modular kit I commissioned from another team member, and I made the joint connectors. Each finished room below has its concept in the corner and its purpose in the level, in play order.

The Geist

The alien began as a programmer’s first pass, before the design had a clear vision. We started over with sea life as the inspiration, aiming for something ethereal, freaky and overall weird.

I textured the Geist, built its dithered shader, and rebuilt its locomotion with IK stepping, soft-body physics and an eye-stem IK. Tobin Pedersen designed, modeled and rigged it.

Production

I co-owned the timeline with the other leads, scoped the game to an MVP and cut luxury features. We broke the work into milestones and tracked it on a Gantt timeline and a Trello board. As design lead, I managed four artists, and most of their tasks started with a design brief I wrote.

Postmortem

State of Entropy is still in development, aiming for a demo release in 2027. Over the course of the project, we’ve had a multitude of things to look back on. These are the main takeaways from our postmortems, from the summer 2025 prototype through spring 2026.

What worked

  • We went from a chaotic workflow to milestones, sprints, clear roles and productive meetings.
  • We committed to a full zero-gravity experience to set us apart from traditional horror thrillers.
  • As design lead, I kept tasks realistic, checked in weekly and gave quick feedback. That got the art pipeline running smoothly, with aesthetic updates every week.
  • Regular playtesting helped us adapt the design to how players actually played, from the controls to how the level guides you.
  • We hit our core goals of a fleshed-out level one built entirely from scratch and a working in-game Geist.

What didn’t

  • Scope creep was a consistent issue, especially as the team grew. The game we first pitched ran 30–45 minutes, so we cut it to a 5–10 minute target, and a “simple” tutorial level still took four milestones instead of one.
  • Early on, roles weren’t defined. It was a wild west of whoever wanted to work on whatever, so important items ended up falling by the wayside.
  • We sometimes gave direction before we knew what we actually wanted, which led to generic results we had to redo.
  • Some cross-team decisions dragged on, so our pivots came later than they should have.

What I’d do differently

  • Do proper preproduction at the start of a project, covering a style guide, a merge process, communication and task assignment.
  • Cut scope early, on both the big picture and individual tasks.
  • Don’t put the cart before the horse. Know what something needs to be in your game before giving direction on it.
  • Treat leadership as dynamic when you’re balancing multiple roles. Some weeks it’s 20% of the job and some weeks it needs 80%, so you can’t lock it at one amount.
  • Clarify decisions in detail, and ask questions early instead of late.
